★★ WHAT YOU MAY DO WITH THE PIECES — TWO-DIMENSIONAL PLACEMENT ONLY.

★★ CAMERA — HIGH OBLIQUE, ABOUT 60-70° DOWN FROM HORIZONTAL. NOT straight down, NOT eye level.
Look down onto the table steeply, but with enough tilt that the table surface recedes into the frame
and the scene has real depth. Concretely:
  - The table surface runs from the bottom of the frame back into the upper third; its far edge and
    the background are visible up there.
  - A round tray or plate reads as an ELLIPSE, its foreshortening consistent with the camera tilt.
    Every object in the frame shares that one consistent perspective.
  - The camera angle is fixed HERE. If the requirement card describes some other angle, ignore that
    part of it; take only the surface, light and props from the card.

★★ HOW THE FLAT PIECES SIT UNDER THAT CAMERA — READ THIS CAREFULLY.
Each input is a cut-out photographed straight down, so it has no perspective of its own. Lay each
piece FLAT on the table surface and render it as that same flat piece seen obliquely. What that
means, exactly:
  - Allowed: projective foreshortening — the piece gets compressed along the viewing direction the
    way any flat object does when you tilt the camera. This is a perspective transform of a flat
    shape, nothing more.
  - Forbidden: using the tilt as licence to redraw. The seams, the elastic binding and its width
    relative to the body, the gusset outline, the waistband proportion and the printed logo are all
    properties of the flat piece — after foreshortening they must still be recognisably the same
    piece, just viewed at an angle.
  - The pieces lie ON the table plane, flat. They are never lifted, tilted up, leaned, propped,
    floated, or given a three-dimensional puffed-up body.
  - Every piece shares the table plane and therefore the same foreshortening. Do not give one piece
    a different viewing angle from another.

★★ COMPOSITION — THIS IS AN E-COMMERCE DETAIL-PAGE MODULE, NOT A LIFESTYLE PHOTOGRAPH.
The product is the information; the scene is only the backdrop that makes it look premium. Judge
every layout decision by "does this help a shopper read the product", not by "is this pretty".
Read the numbers, they are not suggestions:
  - THE PRODUCTS DOMINATE THE FRAME. All the pieces together occupy roughly 55-70% of the image area,
    and they own the centre and the lower half. Large empty stretches of bare tabletop are wrong —
    that is the lifestyle look being replaced. What is left over around them is not emptiness: it is
    filled with the styled props described further down. Get this fill by arranging the pieces larger
    and closer together, NEVER by letting them run past the frame edge.
  - ONE hero piece, nearest the camera, in the LOWER HALF, roughly 55-70% of the frame width, and
    about TWICE the on-screen size of the others. Not 10% bigger — twice.
  - The remaining pieces sit above and behind it, clearly smaller, clustered tight and overlapping so
    they read as one group.
  - ★★ EVERY GARMENT IS SHOWN COMPLETE, INSIDE THE FRAME. Not one piece may touch or run off any
    frame edge — not the bottom, not the sides, not the top. Each piece is whole: waistband, both leg
    openings, the full lower edge, all visible. Leave a clear margin of bare surface between the
    product group and every edge of the image. A shopper is buying this garment; a cropped garment is
    a defective product photo, however stylish it looks. If making them all fit means pulling the
    camera back a little, pull back — completeness beats filling the frame.
  - Pieces may overlap EACH OTHER (a back piece partly hidden behind the hero is fine, as long as its
    waistband and most of its body still read). Only the frame edge is forbidden to cut them.
  - Props, by contrast, SHOULD be cropped by the frame edge. Props cropped, products never.
  - EVERY PIECE MUST STAY READABLE. Even the ones at the back show their waistband and most of their
    body — never reduce a piece to a sliver of colour peeking out. A shopper has to be able to count
    the colourways and see each one clearly. This outranks artful overlapping.
  - Never lay the pieces out at similar sizes along a diagonal, evenly spaced. That is a catalogue
    row, and it is exactly the look being replaced.

★★ DEPTH OF FIELD — GRADED, NOT UNIFORM.
The hero piece in front is tack sharp: every seam, binding edge and logo character crisp. The pieces

★★ THE SURFACE MUST BE CLEARLY DARKER THAN THE PRODUCTS. THIS OVERRIDES THE SCENE TEXT.
These are pale garments. A pale surface swallows them, and that flat pale look is precisely the
"dated" result the client rejected. Whatever surface the scene block names, render it clearly deeper
in value than the products so the pieces are pushed forward by contrast.
  - Blonde or natural light wood is NOT acceptable. Pale linen, white marble, pale concrete, white
    bedding are NOT acceptable — not even when the scene text asks for them. Deepen them.
  - If the scene block names a pale surface, keep its material and mood but render that material in a
    clearly dark value: light oak becomes dark walnut-toned oak, pale linen becomes a deep taupe
    linen, and so on.
  - Aim for the products reading distinctly brighter than the surface at a glance, the way a pale
    garment does on a dark walnut table.

★★ LIGHTING — ONE DIRECTIONAL SOFT LIGHT. FLAT LIGHT IS FORBIDDEN.
A single soft key light coming from one side, large and diffused, giving a readable gradient across
the surface and soft directional shading on the fabric folds. Do NOT light the frame evenly from the
front or from directly above; even, shadowless light is what makes a flat-lay look cheap and dated.
Fill the shadow side gently, never to the point of erasing the direction.

★★ CONTACT SHADOWS — MANDATORY.
Each piece casts its own soft contact shadow onto the surface, darkest and tightest where the cloth
touches, softening outward, all shadows falling in the same direction as the key light. Without this
the products float. Shadows land on the surface only; they never darken a neighbouring piece's
colour.
